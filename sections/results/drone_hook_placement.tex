\section{Drone hook placement}
\label{sec:dronehook}
This section focuses on analyzing how the UAVs attach to the payload. First, we compare a common cable-routing scheme across all configurations shown in Figure \ref{fig:3uavsConnections}, keeping every other design variable fixed. This isolates the influence of the attachment topology itself. Lastly, we rerun the prescreening and subsequent simulations to see if these results also apply to the best performing configurations in the prescreening.

\subsection{Stewart cable routing}
The Stewart cable routing is adopted as the reference architecture throughout the remainder of this chapter. The complete numerical results are reported in Tables \ref{tab:tracking_all_configurations}, \ref{tab:performance_all_configurations}, and \ref{tab:dexterity_all_configurations} in Appendix \ref{app:UAVconnectionSimulation}.

Figure \ref{fig:pose_reached} shows the evolution of pose reachability as progressively richer UAV attachment geometries are introduced. The point attachment is unable to reach any target pose, while the aligned, line, disaligned, and diagonal layouts provide only modest improvements. The Stewart configuration reached approximately 65\% of the target poses. Pairwise McNemar tests showed that this improvement in pose reachability is statistically significant for all competing layouts.

A point attachment allows the three UAVs to apply forces through the same location, producing almost no independent rotational authority. Constraining the attachment points to a line or to nearly collinear configurations restores only a subset of the available moments. As the attachment geometry becomes increasingly two-dimensional, the UAV subsystem acquires greater ability to generate arbitrary six-dimensional wrenches. The triangular attachment provides independent moment arms in two dimensions, allowing the UAV subsystem to contribute effectively to the generation of both forces and moments acting on the payload. This increased wrench-generation capability explains the substantial improvement in task completion.

Besides reaching more poses, the Stewart routing also produced the lowest position and orientation errors, and it exhibited the smallest percentage of cable rubbing events (31.7\%), indicating that the routing naturally reduces cable interference during the simulated tasks.

The statistical analysis confirms that the Stewart routing provides the highest overall task performance. It achieved the largest mean composite score (5.57), substantially outperforming the diagonal, disaligned and aligned layouts, while the difference with the line configuration was not statistically significant. 

From a force-generation perspective, the Stewart configuration achieved the highest capacity margin, the largest worst-case capacity margin, and the greatest radius of the available force set. These results indicate that the architecture maintains a larger safety margin from actuator saturation while preserving greater capability to generate the required payload wrench.

On the other hand, the Stewart routing does not maximize all geometric performance indices. Higher conditioning index and manipulability values were obtained by the aligned and disaligned layouts. Nevertheless, these theoretical advantages did not translate into better execution of the manipulation task, as those configurations exhibited substantially poorer pose tracking and lower success rates. This result highlights that maximizing purely kinematic indices does not necessarily maximize task performance. Although conditioning index and manipulability are useful during preliminary design, they should be interpreted together with task-oriented metrics such as pose reachability, force margins, tracking accuracy, and cable interference.

These results justify selecting the Stewart routing as the baseline architecture for the remainder of the UAV hook-placement study. The following sections therefore investigate the influence of the UAV attachment topology while maintaining the Stewart cable routing fixed.

\subsection{Ground Screening Across UAV Attachment Topologies}
\label{sec:ground_screening_uav_topologies}

Before evaluating the different UAV attachment topologies, each candidate payload-ground layout was first passed through the same ground-only pre-screening procedure described in Section \ref{sec:ground_screening} and Algorithm \ref{alg:cable_optimization}. Since this stage operates exclusively on the ground-cable Jacobian $J_{p,\text{ground}}$, it is, by construction, independent of the UAV attachment geometry. 

This is proved by \texttt{rectangle-opposite-2X3}, which is screened under diagonal, line, and triangle topologies and returns the identical routing in all three cases. By contrast, \texttt{two-triangles-vertical-parallelepiped}, the only layout screened under all six topologies, splits into two distinct routings: aligned, disaligned, line, and triangle all select the same routing, while diagonal and point instead select an alternative one. The same pattern is observed for \texttt{rhombus-ext-2X3} and \texttt{rectangle-opposite-pyramid}. In every case where the selected routing differs, the reported indices differ by a small margin; whenever the routing matches exactly, the indices agree.

As shown in Table \ref{tab:prescreening_feasibility_all} in Appendix \ref{app:full_results}, the pre-screening returned feasible across all six UAV topologies (point, aligned, disaligned, line, diagonal, and triangle).  As in the Stewart baseline analysis of Section \ref{sec:ground_screening}, Wrench Feasibility remains the dominant source of routing rejection, accounting for the large majority of routing-pose failures, while WCC violations are comparatively rare.

The resulting best routings (shown in Table \ref{tab:prescreening_UAVs} inside Appendix \ref{app:full_results}) are numerically similar to the triangle baseline reported in Table \ref{tab:prescreening_index}: the conditioning index of the selected routing lies in the range $\nu \approx 0.068$-$0.085$, the smallest singular value in $\sigma_{\min} \approx 0.163$-$0.204$, and manipulability in $w \approx 0.024$-$0.096$, regardless of which UAV topology the layout is ultimately screened under. Table \ref{tab:prescreening_by_topology} summarizes the best-scoring layout obtained for each topology.

\subsection{Point}
The point attachment configuration is never able to command the payload to reach the desired pose, yielding 0 out of 60 successful poses across all setups that employ this drone-placement strategy. This shows that using a shared drone attachment point is a bad design choice, independent from the ground routing it is paired with.

A good way of seeing this limitation is obtained by comparing the same ground cable routing with different drone attachments. For example, contrasting \texttt{line-line-triangle} with \texttt{line-line-point} shows the reachability dropping from 40.00 \% to 0.00\%. Cable rubbing is also near-universal (76.7-100\%, vs. 31.7\% for Stewart) and the capacity margin is negative for every point configuration, meaning the average-pose wrench requirement is not even statically possible with strictly positive tensions.

This is the physically expected consequence of collapsing all three UAV cables onto a single shared attachment point: the drones loose their ability to generate an independent moment about that point, so orientation and fine position control fall almost entirely on the six ground cables. These results show that point-type drone attachment is not an idea and should be left out of the automated architecture search.

\subsection{Aligned}
As shown in Table \ref{tab:performance_all_configurations}, \texttt{two-triangles-vertical-parallelepiped-aligned} achieves the highest composite score of the whole group $N=6.9394$, which is about 25\% higher than the Stewart baseline (5.5706). However, a p-value of 0.94 shows us how this difference is not statistically significant. Conditioning index and manipulability are significantly better than Stewart for every aligned routing, but this does not translate into closed-loop tracking. When we look at the average of the five ground routings, the aligned attachment only reaches 3.7 \% of target poses, result comparable with the point attachment even though the composite scores are on average as good as or better than Stewarts. Furthermore, cable rubbing is also extreme, averaging 90.7\% across the five routings (Table \ref{tab:meanValues}).

The aligned case shows how conditioning index and composite score, being static / quasi-steady quantities, can actively mislead the search toward geometrically "efficient" but practically uncontrollable UAV attachments.

The aligned configuration distributes the UAV attachment points along a single edge of the payload. This geometry improves the conditioning index and manipulability compared with the Stewart layout, indicating a more uniform Jacobian. However, the reduced spatial distribution of the lifting forces limits the ability to generate restoring moments about all axes. As a consequence, despite favorable geometric indices, the dynamic simulations show poor pose reachability and relatively large position and orientation errors.

\subsection{Disaligned}
Like the aligned attachment, the disaligned configuration shows the same qualitative behavior, but in a less extreme way. Conditioning index and manipulability improve significantly over Stewart for all five routings. In contrast, capacity margin and worst-case capacity margin are generally lower, even if not always significantly. 

Even so, while the two best-performing disaligned routings are statistically indistinguishable from Stewart on the composite score, none of the five routings performs well on task completion, succeding only 13\% of the time, which is the second-best average among the non-triangular topologies, behind diagonal. Lastly, cable rubbing dominates.


\subsection{Line}
Line attachment produces the largest composite-score gains of any topology examined. Both \texttt{rectangle-\allowbreak opposite-\allowbreak 2X3-\allowbreak line} (8.6194) and \texttt{parallelepiped-\allowbreak int-\allowbreak 2X3-\allowbreak line}\\ (8.2924) score 55\% and 49\% above Stewart, respectively. However, these improvements are not significant because the results vary a lot from one pose to another. The 6.7\% average of task completion is significantly lower than the Stewart configuration, placing it above aligned and point but below diagonal and disaligned.

One routing is diagnostically notable: \texttt{rhombus-ext-2X3-line}. It is the only line variant whose cable-rubbing rate (46.7\%) is not significantly different from Stewart's (31.7\%, p = 0.093), while the other four routings range from 93.3\% to 100\% rubbing. Combined with its highest pose-reached rate in the group (20.0\%), this suggests that within the line family, it is the least disruptive pairing, though still far below the triangular baseline.

As discussed in Section \ref{sec:degenerativeConfigurationa}, constraining attachment points along a single axis inherently limits achievable moment directions, regardless of whether those constrained points lie on the ground or aerial subsystem.

\subsection{Diagonal}
Among the non-triangular UAV attachment topologies, the diagonal configuration provides the best overall dynamic performance. Across its five ground routings, it achieves an average pose reachability of 14.7\%, the highest of all the alternative attachment geometries investigated. Nevertheless, this performance remains well below that of the triangular attachment, whose average pose reachability is approximately 65\%. 

The best individual routing, \texttt{rhombus-ext-2x3-diagonal}, reaches 30.0\% of the target\ poses, approximately twice the average best performance obtained with the other non-triangular topologies. Although this is the strongest result within the diagonal family, it still reaches less than half of the poses achieved by the ACTS Stewart baseline. Interestingly, it is also the only diagonal configuration whose capacity margin (2.9175) is statistically indistinguishable from that of the Stewart platform (2.7714, p=0.860), while exhibiting a slightly higher nominal value.

As observed for the line and aligned attachment geometries, the static composite score does not reliably predict the dynamic behavior. \\ For instance, \texttt{rectangle-opposite-2x3-diagonal} achieves the highest composite score among all configurations evaluated in this section (8.9172), yet exhibits the lowest pose reachability within the diagonal family (3.3\%). This discrepancy further confirms that favorable geometric indices alone are insufficient to characterize the closed-loop performance of the system.


\subsection{Triangle comparison analysis}

Among all evaluated UAV attachment topologies, the triangular configuration consistently provides the best compromise between static wrench quality and dynamic task execution. Unlike the alternative layouts, it is the only attachment geometry that simultaneously achieves high pose reachability, low cable-rubbing rates, and accurate position and orientation tracking.

Although several non-triangular topologies obtain comparable values of conditioning index, manipulability, or composite score, these improvements do not translate into successful task execution. In particular, the point attachment demonstrates that static metrics alone may be misleading: despite exhibiting one of the largest theoretical force capabilities for some ground layouts, it fails to reach a single target pose because the common attachment point severely limits the payload's rotational controllability.

These results indicate that the geometric arrangement of the UAV attachment points is substantially more influential than the precise choice of ground routing. Forming a triangle distributes the aerial forces over the payload surface, allowing the UAV subsystem to generate independent forces and moments in all directions while maintaining good cable separation. Consequently, the triangular attachment is adopted as the reference architecture for all subsequent analyses and is retained as the only UAV topology considered during the automated architecture search.

Among the three triangular attachment geometries (aligned, disaligned and diagonal), the diagonal configuration consistently provides the best compromise between tracking performance, force generation and cable interference. The aligned topology achieves the highest conditioning index and manipulability, whereas the disaligned configuration slightly improves the force generation capabilities. However, neither advantage translates into better execution of the manipulation task.

These observations reinforce the conclusions drawn in Section 6.5.1. While geometric performance indices remain valuable indicators during the preliminary design stage, they cannot alone predict the practical behaviour of the system. Metrics directly related to the task, including pose reachability, tracking accuracy and cable interference, provide a more reliable assessment of the overall architecture.

Among the alternative UAV attachment layouts, the diagonal topology appears to be the most promising. Nevertheless, none of the evaluated configurations surpasses the Stewart routing, which remains the preferred solution for the considered façade manipulation scenario.
